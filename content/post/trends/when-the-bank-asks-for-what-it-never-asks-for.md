# When the Bank Asks for What It Says It Never Asks For
---

A customer tries to make a legitimate online payment. It's declined: the card has been blocked as a precaution.

They call the bank. Several minutes of menus later, they ask for a person. No security agent is available, so the system promises a callback.

The callback comes — from a different number. Everything fits in timing and context, so the customer picks up. After confirming their name, the agent asks for **the full card number**.

The customer hesitates. The answer: *that's the procedure. If you don't trust it, you can call back and start again.*

## The paradox: an insecure protocol to prevent fraud

For years, banks have taught customers one rule: **never give your card details to anyone who calls you, even if they say they're your bank.** Many publish the list of things they will never ask for by phone — starting with the card number.

And then the bank calls through that very channel to ask for exactly that.

**The damage isn't the request itself. It's the lesson: the bank is teaching its customers that sharing card details with whoever calls is normal — which is precisely the script fraudsters use.**

This isn't the agent's fault. They are almost certainly following the procedure they were given. The problem is the design:

* → Block a legitimate online payment out of caution.
* → Resolve it over the phone, the least verifiable channel.
* → Ask on that channel for data the bank itself says it never requests.

> Security that contradicts its own rules isn't security. It's a habit.

## What the bank really loses

A customer who refuses to give their details doesn't insist: it isn't safe, and they've already lost too much time. They complete the purchase with another card from another bank.

The bank didn't lose a payment. It lost the customer's trust — and very likely the card along with it. It won't show up as a complaint. It shows up as a card that stops being used.

## The channel already exists

The same customer has the bank's app on their phone, already authenticated. It can confirm a payment in one tap. It could carry the conversation too — by voice, video or chat — with the customer identified before a word is said, and without anyone reading a card number aloud.

🤔 **If the app can confirm a payment in one click, why is the phone still part of the security protocol?**
