# Move the Capacity, Not the Customer
---

In person, the usual and expected thing is that the person who will serve us is where the customer needs them.

It's so obvious we don't even think about it. It's exactly what we expect when we go to a branch to deal with something that feels too complex, too sensitive or too awkward for a digital channel. A brand's entire physical footprint is built on that assumption: the branch network, the opening hours, the queue management.

And yet that is precisely where the customer runs into this:

* → *"There's quite a wait at this time of day."*
* → *"Sorry, we can't handle that here today."*
* → *"Please come back tomorrow."*
* → *"It's better to call the contact center to start that process."*
* → *"You'd need to book an appointment on the website or the app."*
* → *"Sorry, the person who handles that had to step out."*

**Behind each of those sentences there is no complaint.**

The customer almost never says anything. They don't argue, they don't file a claim, they don't fill in a survey. They leave — and they may not try that route again, or recommend it to anyone.

That is what our report *The CX Gap in Assisted Service* calls **silent abandonment**, and it is the hardest loss for an organization to see today, because it doesn't show up on any dashboard.

## The constraint has already gone — even if the design hasn't changed

The assumption is no longer necessary. Today an organization's experts can see, show, resolve and process cases in several places at once, without travelling. Their capacity to serve is no longer tied to the building they sit in.

And yet service is still designed the same way. Not by mistake: because it worked that way for decades, because competitors do the same and nobody loses relative position, and because that's what "business as usual" means. Nobody checks the foundations while the building is still standing — even when "being there" no longer matters, and the experience is visibly deteriorating.

> The question isn't which AI to rush into your digital channels while the brand's flagship channel degrades. It's what you would do differently if you assumed service is no longer digital *or* physical, but both at once.

## Invert the order

Instead of moving the customer to where the capacity is, move the capacity to where the customer already is.

* ✅ The branch stops being the limit and becomes an access point to remote expertise.
* ✅ The expert — supported by AI, if needed — reaches the customer who is already waiting, instead of sending them somewhere else.
* ✅ The visit the customer chose to make ends in a resolution, not in a redirection.

That is what *Phygital* means. Digitalizing isn't sending the customer off into cyberspace. It's making sure the person who knows reaches them, wherever they already are.

## Where to start this week

If you can only do one thing, do this: for five days, count how many in-person visits end with one of those six sentences. No survey, no project, no budget. Just the count, by hand if necessary.

That number appears in no CX report, and it is usually the most uncomfortable one of all — because it doesn't measure what the customer thinks. It measures what the organization couldn't resolve on the day the customer decided to come in person.

And it is almost always where everything starts. The simple requests are already going to the bot; your customer knows that without being told again at the branch.

💡 **How many of your in-person visits this week will end in "please come back tomorrow"?**
