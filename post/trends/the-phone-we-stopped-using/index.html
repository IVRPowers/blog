The Phone We Stopped Using — Except in Every Hotel Room

That landline is still there, in every hotel room, whatever the category. Sometimes there are even two — one on the nightstand, one in the bathroom. Younger guests have already made up their minds about it: they no longer know how to use it, or want to.

Street phone booths disappeared entirely, and nobody missed them. Office desks lost their landlines years ago — everything moved to the laptop or the phone in someone’s pocket. And yet in the hotel room, it’s still sitting there, as if no one had ever stopped to question it.

Why? Because an industry that renovates ultra-modern buildings, installs expensive furniture, and rethinks styles and experiences without pause let itself be guided by one established rule — and never came back to question it. Today’s guest arrives with a brand-new smartphone already in hand, and the room has WiFi. That landline no longer signals luxury: it subtracts minimalism, with its cable in plain sight, and a guest who actually wants to use it to request something is more likely to feel like they’re imposing. It resists the room’s transformation like a piece that no longer fits.

What’s lost with it isn’t a device. It’s the opportunity for digital convergence. What’s kept is a habit whose original purpose no longer holds up.

If the landline were really there for safety, it would also be in the hallways and by the pool — and those same needs are already covered there, in other ways. Which leaves habit as the only real explanation for a fixture that survived every other renovation.

Lower-cost alternatives already exist:

  • ✅ A QR code that opens a PWA
  • ✅ An app built into the room’s own Smart TV
  • ✅ An integrated tablet, for luxury environments
  • ✅ A full Phygital system for the hotel

Phone booths were retired the moment they stopped making sense, and no one grieved them. The hotel landline is taking a lot longer to go — but some regulations would already accept it “disappearing,” as long as it’s replaced by something better. That change isn’t going to be forced by a regulation. It has to come from the industry itself.
